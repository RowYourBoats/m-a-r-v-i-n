This essay is a digestion of Miéville's 2000 novel of the city Perdido Street Station, paired with images from Karl von Frisch's Animal Architecture (1974).

—

In the opening of Perdido Street Station we have Tolkien’s Bird’s Eye View brought into the slop and grime of a real city through our framing narrator, the dewinged Garuda bird-man Yagharek. We meet him on a barge coming from the sterile desert of the Cymek entering the city through its underbelly. “The rotting buildings lean against each other, exhausted.[…] I wonder how this city looks from above, no chance for the city to hide then, if you came at it on the wind you would see it from miles and miles away like a dirty smear, like a slab of carrion thronging with maggots.” We are introduced to the city through the eyes of the vagrant, the stateless, the outcast. Contrast that with:

"In a hole in the ground there lived a hobbit. Not a nasty, dirty, wet hole, filled with the ends of worms and an oozy smell... it was a hobbit-hole, and that means comfort." — J.R.R. Tolkien, The Hobbit 

In Tolkien’s legendarium we follow the adventures of his heroes and we see a landscape unfold with great vistas, natural wonder, and great mournful symbols of human endeavour rising as if carved from this splendid grubless natural world. And yet Tolkien brings us into the world of the long defeat. In his Gondor we find civilizations past have come to define place and identity for a people ravaged by illness and war unable to bear the burden of the architectural memories left them. People entombed in roles set for them centuries or millennia ago. Trying to revive the memory of a once glorious age. Tolkien didn’t show us the Great Game of politics pitting young men against each other but he showed us the gravestones of that era in his Gondor. His diminutive hero Frodo ends up going through the trenches and vileness of industrial war trying to cut one of the strings connecting the old world with the new. And, although it might be too generous a reading of Tolkien’s Lord of the Rings, he showed us the human cost of being born into a decaying imperial dream with each age being a lower high. 

Tolkien’s beautifully articulated worlds have come to define countless games, films, comics, and novels that came after. Perhaps more than a reaction to the book itself Miéville’s Perdido Street Station was very much a reaction to the influence of the book. A reaction to the cherry picking of the novel and the proposed underlying order that Tolkien proposed as underlying the fight against the machine as it seemingly stratified new tropes of fantasy and fairy tales. In Miéville’s own words: "Tolkien is rural and bucolic, so let's make it urban and shitty; Tolkien is feudalism lite, so let's make it capitalism dark." 

New Crobuzon is dotted with blistering slabs of habitation ten, twenty, thirty storeys high. Industrial and residential. Underground and airborne. Where some stories would weave great myths around 200ft ribs, ancient, bleached, and stony sticking out of the ground in Perdido Street Station it’s the location of Bonetown, “a shabby place where nothing good lives." This isn’t a city that is a singular intent carved into a landscape or a city lost in the memory of a glorious long forgotten age. Instead New Crobuzon is a city defined only by its uncontrollable activity and memories have been submerged in competing architectural identities. It’s a city of looming architectures, garbage dumps, upstream leisure boating and downstream grime, alleys, clandestine bars. Miéville sketches a city that wields parks as upper- and middle-class barriers, alleys as funnels for violence, and everyone being able to read who belongs where through scent, dress, language, and the way they carry themselves. People switch allegiances, change goals, abandon and pick up projects, and a city that is so big and metabolic that change is constant. Even the markets, food smells, and noisy conversations giving a street purpose on one day will be gone the next and leave only silence, detritus, and a sense of unease. Miéville takes us through a city where power is not top-down, right-left, but rather an intricate web where everyone needs to deal with everyone and everyone is expendable in service of the powerful engine that is New Crobuzon. And through it all we find a city that is always moving. A city where stories are told, myths are made, all the while living amongst the architectural detritus of stories and myths of old. “The tons of concrete and tar that constituted the city covered ancient geography, knolls and barrows and verges, undulations that were still visible.”  

For Tolkien his legendarium was worldbuilding through Norse mythology and language. In Perdido Street Station Miéville borrows from Egyptian, Slavic, and other mythologies while language is being used in its political reality form. For Miéville even the people who grow up in a language can have barriers to being considered an insider. He shows us the Garuda bird people who grew up in New Crobuzon, the Spatters ghetto, having physical barriers to making the full range of sounds necessary for human language. And we are reminded that even the humans living beside them in the ghetto use language to mark insider and outsider. Then there are the khepri, female bodies with beetle heads, who communicate richly and broadly with each other through pheromones, but can only communicate through sign language and writing with those not like them. Other species are briefly touched on as having different ties to language or even reality, like the spider-like reality-threading Weavers. But amongst all these street level interactions we have the bigger looming threat of the city’s political engine. An engine that only looks at what use can be made of people. Identity is a street level reality that can be used to placate, differentiate, and pit against each other those in the same circumstances. We don’t see discrimination as blind hate, but rather wielded as an incisive weapon. 

Miéville’s city of New Crobuzon is a city of innumerable strangers, roles, and lives living in and sustaining an interconnected  web of architectures, languages, realities, and identities. 

And in this balance of economic pragmatism, political reality and survivability Miéville brings us through both place-making and place-binding. In New Crobuzon home can be identity, sanctuary, prison, shackle, gathering place, and more. Place marks your path in life and even though you can escape it you can only do so by finding a new place in the city. But you’ll need to look for or make the communities, the nooks and crannies of the city that allow you to transform yourself. One of the strongest aspects of the novel is its ability to show the violence of place. Every time we are brought into an area we are shown both the winners and losers. Those deemed useful by their own communities, their new city’s other inhabitants and those who belong to neither. 

And if you’re not useful to the city, or worse yet, a detriment to the city’s health. Then the city can either mark you as an outcast or force you to become labor for the city through a process called remaking. The city can graft flesh, animal or human, xenian or machine, useful or not useful, but always a mockery and stigma onto a person’s body to punish them. We have the crane remade, the piston remade, those standing on telescoping tripod legs, and even remade that are facing backwards to avoid looking at the main threat in the book, the hypnotizing predatorial slake moths.  We find at its most darkly grotesque the thief turned sideshow freak Garuda with feathers of all shapes shoved ruthlessly into his skin in a thick uneven insulating layer with a large and stained beak affixed like a moving gas mask and tiny rotting wings almost soldered onto the muscles of his back. A human thief left with no place in society except telling tall tales of a life he never lived while literally, and viscerally according to the smell, rotting away. And in the same grotesqueness those who choose to be remade, who own the stigma and make it their own, like the crime boss Motley. A remade of skin, fur, feathers, antlers, feelers, numerous eyes and mouths, cloven hooves and reptile feet. “Scales gleamed. Fins quivered.Wings fluttered brokenly.” He had remade himself into a grotesque form too complex to take in,feeling like he could reorganize the shards of his physiognomy overnight. And in Motley we get the author speaking with clarity of his thoughts on the city. “I believe this to be the fundamental dynamic. Transition. The point where one thing becomes another. The zone where the disparate become part of the whole. The hybrid zone.”

New Crobuzon is not only too big to be owned by any one form. One architecture. One power. It’s not even owned by one time or one story. It’s a nametag on pile of buildings leaning on each other. “Even the trains that moved [...] around New Crobuzon’s great carcass fought to stay above the houses, as if they were afraid of the putrefaction of architecture.” Its inhabitants' fragmented bodies continually shatter the city’s architecture to fit their Xenian, non-mammalian hybrid bodies. Half-bird/half-human, scurrying scarab males/scarab head females, half-khepri/half-ape, sentient cacti, bulbous frog humanoids, predator moths, and numerous barely named, but not shown, ones. All with agency, bodies that require fitting, needs to be met, stories to be told, gods to be worshipped, and wants to be filled under the shared chimneyscape of New Crobuzon. This is a world where everything has coordinates in space and time, and where access is defined not by existence alone but by the capital required to reach, research, and apply it. In Perdido Street Station we’re taken through a city that is a negotiation. A negotiation always ready to unravel and to be renegotiated through capital and power.

The Glasshouse is a giant glass and steel dome that serves as the climate controlled home of the cactus people, the cactacae. The dome quite literally carves a space for the cactacae into the city. Leading to a snarled puzzle cartography with road-ends, rumps of avenues, corner of a park, stump of a canal, half of a house as they were cut off from the city by the edge of the dome. It’s a prison palace with additional buildings wedged in between the cut-off appendages, and yet it is lush with desert and veldt vegetation with a wide thoroughfare made into a vegetable garden. Elders and the strong of their community preserve a way of life that is disconnected from what it was before the dome and what their people are outside of the dome in the Cymek. The dome exists both within the city and on its periphery. By their own choice and by the political establishment’s endorsement. The cactacae laborers exist in an exchange of labor and reward without engaging with the city that they live in. A choice not available to two-thirds of the cactacae New Crobuzonites — the Glasshouse outcasts, those who didn't fit anymore or never had a place in it. They can get temporary access through the airlocks to shop, pray, and briefly feel at home in the parks. But they must exit before sundown. Those cactacae are left to fend for themselves on the border of their sanctuary within a new community of near outcasts or out in the broader city having to find or create communities for themselves. The outcasts transform both themselves and the city that they live in. All the while inside the dome their customs and culture ossify within the increasingly deteriorating glass and steel frame. “Here in this tightly enclosed space there was no gust to carve patterns in the sand and the desert landscape had to be wrought by hand.”

The reason behind why things are the way they are is not made clear from an apocryphal mythologizing point of view but we can find biological rationale, rituals, and their architectural results throughout the book. A city where “Some other intelligence has made these human streets their own.” In this case referring to the khepri whose biology is a mix of female human bodies with scarabs for heads with males nothing more than small mindless scuttler. The khepri ended up fleeing from their home two generations ago and have used their ability to spit sculpt (improperly named as mentioned in the novel) have made once human streets their own. Through their own ability to make intricate sculptures and by using their rhino-sized home-grubs who excrete phlegm-cement from their abdomen to create intricate twisting passage ways preferred by most khepri tenants. We tour the khepri community through our guide Lin, one of the protagonists, an artist outcast with criticism of the cult she grew up in, her sanctimonious bourgeois sisters, and a strange sense of kinship with a crimelord khepri we never meet. “She breathed easier when the only khepri in the crowds were, like her, outcasts.” she says as she exits the not-quite ghetto of Salacus Fields to the khepri ghetto of Kinken. In the Glasshouse’s privilege and the khepri ghetto community of Kinken, where male khepri scuttle about with abandon, we see communities warping themselves in an attempt to protect and hold on to their old ways. Twisting themselves into an anachronistic version of the cities and communities they came from. With the cactacae we see different forms of integrating with the economy of the city. In the khepri we see a community erasing their cultural memory, for reasons not explained, and descendants retreating into purer religion, a cult-like religion, a more flexible religion, and a loss of religion. All because their asylum seeking ancestors chose not to pass on the story of their history. With Miéville leaving us the unsatisfying task of imagining the rationale for their decision based on a fragmented back story. 

Of the xenians we are introduced to, the New Crobuzon Garuda, bird people with beaks, giant wings, feathers, and a cloaca as we are left to assume from a sentence from Yagharek about defecating on the city. Those who try to make a home and community for themselves in the city do so in the poorest ghetto of the city, Spatters. A neighborhood built from the detritus of New Crobuzon’s abandoned efforts to tame the untameable Rudewood. Currently left to rot out of sight of the clerks, doctors and lawyers who live, a park and an eight-foot ditch removed, in manicured gardens and polite houses. In Spatters the few Garuda that live in the city have made an eyrie for themselves in four abandoned “big” buildings “with paper-covered windows on the bottom floors and wooden boughs poking out at odd angles from the 6th and 7th floor up.”   Living there and in the sky of the city speaking a language not fitted for their bodies. And unlike the Catacae or khepri the Garuda have nothing that the city wants other than their ability to take flight on their own power. The Spatters’ community live lives where predators have to hide from those hungry for the secret of powerful embodied flight. And yet they live like no other in the city, riding currents and winds, perching on rooftops, defended by stairs, and being free to shit on those below them. 

The most metaphorical of the architectures in the book is that of the vodyanoi. Their biology allows them to watercraft, forcing water into any shape that they want for a few weeks. That same biology forces them to live in water for the majority of their time. Sometimes a bar owner will make do with a dirty bath stationed on the bar floor, at other times wealthy vodyanoi design houses to appear human but inside it would have doorways connecting huge rooms above and below water; canal passageways, and sluices refreshing the water. The unlucky lived downstream in the effluent of the city’s industry in sticky-pitch-coated houses that had crumbled into the water. But their watercraft made them especially useful for a city perched on two rivers meeting the sea. A simple skill that can on the one hand be used to create cute ephemeral toy sculptures can be used as a powerful tool of politics if wielded correctly and with conviction. What made the vodyanoi especially useful in the docks of the city helping with salvage and routing of ships made them a political force to be reckoned with too. Able to hunt fish but cutting off the flow of the river they could just as easily stop ships from passing through. In the vodyanoi there is a species whose lives amongst pollution becomes visceral and palpable rather than those who live in air where pollution is ephemeral and deniable. 

But for all those living in the city and making it their own. Struggling to make it work for them in a battle of wills and reality. The book introduces us to a few beings that deny the reality of the architecture in its totality. Turning buildings and its masses of concrete spilling over the city as landscape. We have the giant reality-bending weaver and emergent consciousness of discarded machines in the construct council and the dream-eating, mind-destroying, slake moths. Incredibly powerful beings with their own way of thinking, emphasizing that even the demonspawn of hell thinks alike in the capitalist reality of New Crobuzon. The weaver is only interested in maintaining some abstract unspoken ununderstandable reality threaded web and willing to help at the cost of scissors, snipping sounds, and a couple of left ears. The latter two parasites in a city that shelters them. Unlike Tolkien's Sauron, these monsters aren't external corruptions of an ideal. They're the city's own logic, followed to its conclusion. They live symbiotically  in the city and their survival is contingent on staying on the good side of some, avoiding the bad side of others, and sometimes on just staying lucky. Miéville’s book consistently pays tribute to the in-between spaces where things can happen because they’re out of sight. The temporarily bustling market, sewers, streets corners, rooftops, alleyways, airlocks, muddy rivers, abandoned buildings, taboo neighborhoods and its inhabitants, the rogue scientists, stubborn artists, street urchins, thaumaturges for hire, new life, and ruthless dream eaters. And in his slake moths he celebrates “the subversive topography” that can be realized by literally boring through a city reminiscent of a prison escape, a bank heist, and Gordon Matta-Clark. “It crept at bizarre angles between the walls of the terrace, tight and close, sending the sound of his breath and the clanking of the monkeys’ bouncing into Isaac’s ears.” New Crobuzon is a three-dimensional and porous city behind facades that function as masks. While in the construct council’s chaotically emerging consciousness from the detritus of the city we see an intelligence looking to vociferously gather data to feed itself. Although it’s never made clear that there’s a purpose to the data collection other than knowing more, becoming  more, being more than what it is now. And the Construct Council does so not by creating something new or abusing power. The  council gathers acolytes who want to believe it is something more than what it is telling them it is. The construct council spreads through the invisible underclass of workers that blend into the city efforts to sustain itself. 


And the city endures, not as an architectural ideal, but as the muck and detritus of the life that’s constantly happening inside of it. In New Crobuzon the city outlasts its inhabitants and the role outlasts the person, In New Crobuzon we find city planning and architecture in all its aspirational and violent facets and we see architecture being edited, repurposed and remade into a reflection of the people that live there. Throughout the book we see everything through lenses of precarity and contingency.But we never know if that's a true change of state or just a chapter in an already woven web. Where Tolkien shows an ideal worth defending, Miéville shows a city whose vigor can't be distinguished from appetite. 

Made literal in the remaking of Yagharek in the final chapter. While other characters lose their sense of self and place in the city and are forced to fleeYagharek gives up the sky, his old identity, and the bird's eye view we opened with, embraces his flightlessness, plucks his feathers and descends permanently into the street level. Some continue on. And others leave. And the fleeing Yagharek chooses a home while embracing what he first saw as a mockery of his old self that he initially wanted to overcome. The punishment he wanted to undo. He becomes a more ideal version of the circus freak remade earlier in the book. And so New Crobuzon lives on not as a mythic monument but as the masticating city.
