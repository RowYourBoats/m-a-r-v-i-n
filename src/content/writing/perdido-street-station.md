In the opening of Perdido Street Station we have Tolkien's Bird's Eye View brought into the slop and grime of a real city through our framing narrator, the dewinged Garuda bird-man Yagharek. We meet him on a barge coming from the sterile desert of the Cymek entering the city through its underbelly. "The rotting buildings lean against each other, exhausted.[…] I wonder how this city looks from above, no chance for the city to hide then, if you came at it on the wind you would see it from miles and miles away like a dirty smear, like a slab of carrion thronging with maggots." We are introduced to the city not through the royal procession-like visitors of Lord of the Rings, but through the eyes of the vagrant, the stateless, the outcast. Contrast that with:

"In a hole in the ground there lived a hobbit. Not a nasty, dirty, wet hole, filled with the ends of worms and an oozy smell... it was a hobbit-hole, and that means comfort." — J.R.R. Tolkien, The Hobbit

In Tolkien's legendarium we follow the adventures of his heroes and we see a landscape unfold with great vistas, natural wonder, and great symbols of human endeavour rising as if carved from this splendid natural world. And yet Tolkien brings us into the world of the long defeat. In his Gondor we find civilizations past have come to define place and identity for a people ravaged by illness and war unable to bear the burden of the architecture left them.

Tolkien's worlds have come to define countless games, films, comics, and novels that came after. But for Miéville Perdido Street Station was very much a reaction to the influence of the book. In Miéville's own words: "Tolkien is rural and bucolic, so let's make it urban and shitty; Tolkien is feudalism lite, so let's make it capitalism dark." In Mieville's New Crobuzon we find a city that is a melting pot of status, power, and political realities. And in his novel we follow people who pursue dreams of being more than what they are, fight to keep what they have, and where the architecture reminds us that these lives have played out many times before.

For Tolkien his legendarium was worldbuilding through Norse mythology and language. In Perdido Street Station we find language in its political reality form. For Mievile even the people who grow up in a language can have barriers to being considered fluent. He shows us the Garuda bird people who grew up in New Crobuzon, the Spatters ghetto, having physical barriers to making the full range of sounds necessary for human language. And we are reminded that even the humans living beside them in the ghetto use language to mark insider and outsider. Then there are the Khepri, female bodies with beetle heads, who communicate with each other through pheromones, but can only communicate through sign language and writing with those not like them. Other species are briefly touched on as having different ties to language or even reality, like the spider-like reality-threading Weavers. Or the dream eating moths. Mieville's city of New Crobuzon is a city of innumerable strangers, roles, and lives living in and sustaining an interconnected web of architectures, languages, realities, and identities.

As much as New Crobuzon is a place of story and myth, this is a place of the forgotten and forgetting. We find New Crobuzon under the landmark Ribs, the giant, ancient, bleached, and stony ribcage of a being whose identiy has long been lost. This isn't a city that is a singular intent imposed on a landscape — insert undulating landscape — Instead New Crobuzon is a city that has competing identities in its architectures and is defined only by its uncontrollable activity. It's a city of looming architectures, garbage dumps, upstream rowboats and downstream grime, alleys, clandestine bars. Miéville sketches a city that wields parks as upper- and middle-class barriers, alleys as funnels for violence, clothing as a marker of belonging, and everyone being able to read who belongs where through scent, dress, language, and the way they carry themselves. We find people who switch allegiances, change goals, abandon and pick up projects, and a city that is so big and metabolic that change is constant. Even the markets, food smells, and noisy conversations giving a street purpose on one day will be gone the next and leave only silence, detritus, and a sense of unease. Mieville takes us through a city where power is not top-down, right-left, but rather an intricate web where everyone needs to deal with everyone and everyone is expendable in service of the powerful engine that is New Crobuzon. But through the story we find everyone in a power struggle, everyone expendable, and everyone only a step away from stepping on the wrong part of the web.

And in this balance of economic pragmatism, political reality and survivability Miéville brings us through both place-making and place-binding. In New Crobuzon home can be identity, sanctuary, prison, shackle, gathering place, and more. Place marks your path in life and even though you can escape it you can only do so by finding a new place in the city. But you'll need to find the communities, the nooks and crannies of the city that allow you to transform yourself. One of the strongest aspects of the novel is its ability to show the violence of place. Every time we are brought into an area we are shown both the winners and losers. Those deemed useful by their own communities, their new city's other inhabitants and those who belong to neither.

New Crobuzon is not only too big to be owned by anyone in particular the bodies of its inhabitants fragment the city's architecture to fit their xenian, non-mammalian hybrid, bodies. Half-bird/half-human, scurrying scarab males/scarab head females, sentient cacti, bulbous frog humanoids, predator moths, and numerous barely named, but not shown, ones. All with agency, bodies that require fitting, needs to be met, and wants to be filled under the shared chimneyscape of New Crobuzon. This is a world where everything has coordinates in space and time, and where access is defined not by existence alone but by the capital required to reach, research, and apply it. In Perdido Street Station we're taken through a city that is a negotiation.

The Glasshouse is a giant glass and steel dome that serves as the climate controlled home of the cactus people, the Cactacae. The dome quite literally carves a space for the Cactacae into the city. Leading to a snarled puzzle cartography with road-ends, rumps of avenues, corner of a park, stump of a canal, half of a house as they were cut off from the city by the edge of the dome. It's a prison palace with additional buildings wedged in between the cut-off appendages, and yet it is lush with desert and veldt vegetation with a wide thoroughfare made into a vegetable garden. Elders and the strong of their community preserve a way of life that is disconnected from what it was before the dome and what their people are outside of the dome in the Cymek. The dome exists both within the city and on its periphery. By their own choice and by the political establishment's endorsement. The Cactacae laborers exist in an exchange of labor and reward without engaging with the city that they live in. A choice not available to two-thirds of the Cactacae New Crobuzonites — the Glasshouse outcasts, those who didn't fit anymore or never had a place in it. They can get temporary access through the airlocks to shop, pray, and briefly feel at home in the parks. But they must exit before sundown. Those Cactacae are left to fend for themselves on the border of their sanctuary within a new community of near outcasts or out in the broader city having to find or create communities for themselves. The outcasts transform both themselves and the city that they live in. All the while inside the dome their customs and culture ossify within the increasingly deteriorating glass and steel frame. "Here in this tightly enclosed space there was no gust to carve patterns in the sand and the desert landscape had to be wrought by hand."

The reason behind why things are the way they are is not made clear from an apocryphal mythologizing point of view but we can find biological rationale, rituals, and their architectural results throughout the book. A city where "Some other intelligence has made these human streets their own." In this case referring to the Khepri whose biology is a mix of female human bodies with scarabs for heads with males nothing more than small mindless scuttler. The Khepri ended up fleeing from their home two generations ago and have used their ability to spit sculpt (improperly named as mentioned in the novel) have made once human streets their own. Through their own ability to make intricate sculptures and by using their rhino-sized home-grubs who excrete phlegm-cement from their abdomen to create intricate twisting passage ways preferred by most khepri tenants. We tour the Khepri community through our guide Lin, one of the protagonists, an artist outcast with criticism of the cult she grew up in, her sanctimonious bourgeois sisters, and a strange sense of kinship with a crimelord Khepri we never meet. "She breathed easier when the only khepri in the crowds were, like her, outcasts." she says as she exits the not-quite ghetto of Salacus Fields to the Khepri ghetto of Kinken. In the Glasshouse's privilege and the Khepri ghetto community of Kinken, where male Khepri scuttle about with abandon, we find communities warping themselves in an attempt to protect and hold on to their old ways. Twisting themselves into an anachronistic version of the cities and communities they came from. With the Cactacae we see different forms of integrating with the economy of the city. In the Khepri we see a community erasing their cultural memory, for reasons not explained, and descendants retreating into purer religion, a cult-like religion, a more flexible religion, and a loss of religion. All because their asylum seeking ancestors chose not to pass on the story of their history. With Miéville leaving us the unsatisfying task of imagining the rationale for their decision based on a fragmented back story.

Of the xenians we are introduced to, the New Crobuzon Garuda, bird people with beaks, giant wings, feathers, and a cloaca as we are left to assume from a sentence from Yagharek about defecating on the city. Those who try to make a home and community for themselves in the city do so in the poorest ghetto of the city, Spatters. A neighborhood built from the detritus of New Crobuzon's abandoned efforts to tame the untameable Rudewood. Currently left to rot out of sight of the clerks, doctors and lawyers who live, a park and an eight-foot ditch removed, in manicured gardens and polite houses. In Spatters the few Garuda that live in the city have made an eyrie for themselves in four abandoned "big" buildings "with paper-covered windows on the bottom floors and wooden boughs poking out at odd angles from the 6th and 7th floor up." Living there and in the sky of the city speaking a language not fitted for their bodies. And unlike the Catacae or Khepri the Garuda have nothing that the city wants other than their ability to take flight on their own power. The Spatters' community live lives where predators have to hide from those hungry for the secret of powerful embodied flight. And yet they live like no other in the city, riding currents and winds, perching on rooftops, defended by stairs, and being free to shit on those below them.

The most metaphorical of the architecture's in the book is that of the Vodyanoi. Their biology allows them to watercraeft, forcing water into any shape that they want. The catch being that it needs to be maintained by them every few weeks otherwise it will revert to its liquid state. The Vodjanoi have proven especially useful and powerful in the docks of the city. "A whole gang of them can shape quite a bit of the river. They dig holes in the water down to where spilt cargoes lie on the bottom, so the cranes can hook them." Turning a difficult job of retrieving lost cargo into a mundane task. At the same time they can, and do, block the port by shaping the river in such a way that no ship can pass through without risking falling to the bottom.

This is probably clearest in the case of the Construct Council and the slake moths. Incredibly powerful beings parasitizing the very city that allowed them to emerge while remaining in hiding. Unlike Tolkien's Sauron, these monsters aren't external corruptions of an ideal. They're the city's own logic, followed to its conclusion. They live symbiotically in the city and their survival is contingent on staying on the good side of some, avoiding the bad side of others, and sometimes on just staying lucky. Miéville's book consistently pays tribute to the in-between spaces where things can happen because they're out of sight. The temporarily bustling market, sewers, streets corners, rooftops, alleyways, airlocks, muddy rivers, abandoned buildings, taboo neighborhoods and its inhabitants, the rogue scientists, stubborn artists, street urchins, thaumaturges for hire, new life, and ruthless dream eaters.

And the city endures, not as an architectural ideal, but as the muck and detritus of the life that's constantly happening inside of it. In New Crobuzon the city outlasts its inhabitants and the role outlasts the person, but throughout its history people are finding their life in Miéville's city's terrains and architectures. In New Crobuzon we find city planning and architecture in all its aspirational and violent facets and we see architecture being edited, repurposed and remade into a reflection of the people that live there. Throughout the book we see everything through lenses of precarity and contingency. We see the powerful brought down and the powerless empowered. For a moment at least. But we never know if that's a true change of state or just a chapter in an already woven web. Where Tolkien shows an ideal worth defending, Miéville shows a city whose vigor can't be distinguished from appetite.

Made literal in the remaking of Yagharek in the final chapter. While the characters Isaac, Lin, and Derkhan lose their sense of self and place and are forced to flee the city, not in land but onto the sea. Yagharek gives up the sky, the bird's eye view you opened with, plucks his feathers, embraces his winglessness and descends permanently into the street level. Some continue on. And others leave. And the fleeing Yagharek finds a home while embracing the mockery of his old self that he initially wanted to overcome. Becoming a more ideal version of the circus freak remade earlier in the book. And so New Crobuzon lives on not as a mythic monument but as the masticating city.
